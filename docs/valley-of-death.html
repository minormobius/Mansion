What Is the Valley of Death?

The valley of death is the gap between academic discovery and commercial viability—the period when a promising therapeutic has demonstrated proof-of-concept but isn't yet attractive to pharmaceutical companies or venture capital.

As Nature Reviews Bioengineering describes it: "The valley represents the gap between when a scientist makes a discovery and when that work has reached the point when a pharmaceutical company will license it, or a venture capital firm will invest in it."

The economics are brutal:

Stage Typical Need Who Funds Current Status
Basic Research $100K-$1M NIH, NSF, foundations Under severe pressure
Valley of Death $1M-$20M ??? Crisis zone
Phase I/II $20M-$100M Seed/Series A VC Constrained
Phase III $100M-$500M+ Series B/C/D, pharma Relatively healthy

The question for 2026: is this gap getting better or worse?

Evidence It's Widening

The Federal Funding Collapse

The National Cancer Institute lost over $300 million and hundreds of staff members from January to March 2025. Worse is coming: the proposed FY2026 budget would cut NCI by $2.7 billion—a 37.2% reduction.

The ripple effects are immediate. NIH's SBIR and STTR programs—which funded approximately 5,000 new awards annually and have channeled over $70 billion into small business R&D—expired on October 1, 2025. No new awards are being issued.

The VC Retreat from Early Stage

Crunchbase reports that biotech's share of US startup funding hit just 8% in 2025—"by far the lowest percentage in years" and the lowest in Crunchbase history.

PitchBook's Q3 2025 analysis shows seed-stage deals at a record low 14% of total deals. The capital that does flow is concentrating in later stages—fewer companies getting larger checks.

"The funding environment for private biotechs is in dire shape. Down 65% in the second quarter, the sector continues to wallow in a valley of death." — PharmaVoice

Evidence It's Narrowing

The Late-Stage Recovery

Q3 2025 saw a 70.9% increase in venture financing deal value over Q2. Series D rounds exploded—up 60-fold from Q2, reaching $832M.

The Fed's September 2025 rate cuts helped. But the bigger story is M&A: biopharma M&A exceeded 2024 with 129 deals totaling approximately $138 billion. Big Pharma is buying.

ARPA-H Filling Gaps

ARPA-H—launched in 2022 to fund high-risk biomedical moonshots—has launched 23 programs supporting nearly 150 projects. Unlike NIH's incremental approach, ARPA-H takes on projects "industry sees as too risky."

In October 2025, the EMBODY program announced major awards: ImmunoVec ($40.7M), Kernal Bio consortium ($48M), Tessera Therapeutics ($41.3M). These are exactly the kind of valley-spanning investments the traditional system struggles to make.

Bridges Being Built

UCSF InVent Fund

Philanthropic endowment funding university research to "de-risk" projects before seeking external investment. Keeps projects in-house longer for greater returns.

Result: 18 projects funded, 4 exits over 3 years, including Rezo Therapeutics ($78M Series A)

NCInnovation

North Carolina's $500M state-funded endowment providing non-dilutive grants to university proof-of-concept projects. Backed by $25M+ in private philanthropy.

Result: $5.2M approved across 7 universities for projects from cancer research to lithium refining

Venture Debt

Non-dilutive financing gaining traction. IO Biotech signed €57.5M ($66.7M) with European Investment Bank for melanoma vaccine development.

Signal: Alternative financing models emerging as VC alone can't support deal volume

Pharma-Academia Collaboration

BIO 2025 featured extensive discussion of new partnership models. Industry recognizes it needs earlier access to innovation; academia needs funding.

Trend: "Evolving collaboration models" becoming industry priority

The Casualties

Tempest Therapeutics

Promising Phase II results for first-line hepatocellular carcinoma treatment. Could not secure Phase III funding. Laid off most staff, exploring "strategic alternatives."

Patient impact: HCC patients have delayed or no access to a drug showing "meaningful survival benefits."

Multiple biotech startups with promising Phase II results shuttered or downsized in 2025 after failing to secure Phase III financing. The valley claims companies with proven efficacy, not just speculative science.

"Several biotech startups with promising Phase II results have shuttered or downsized after failing to secure funding for Phase III trials in 2025." — Candid

Synthesis: The Polarization

The valley of death isn't simply widening or narrowing. It's polarizing.

Stage 2025 Trend Implication
Seed / Early Stage Severely constrained Fewer companies entering the pipeline
Valley (proof-of-concept to Phase II) Critical shortage Promising science dies unfunded
Late Stage (Series C/D) Strong recovery Survivors get well-capitalized
Exit (M&A) Record activity Big Pharma buying aggressively

The capital stack has become bimodal. If you can survive to Series D, there's plenty of money. But the path there has never been harder:

  • Federal cuts reduce the pipeline of basic discoveries
  • SBIR/STTR freeze eliminates a key bridge mechanism
  • Seed funding collapse means fewer companies attempt the crossing
  • VC concentration means investors prefer late-stage de-risked bets

The bridges being built—university endowments, ARPA-H, venture debt, pharma partnerships—are real but small relative to the gap. UCSF's InVent Fund has funded 18 projects in 3 years. The NIH SBIR program funded 5,000 annually before it expired.

What This Means

For researchers: The path from bench to bedside is narrower than ever. Alternative mechanisms matter more. ARPA-H applications, university commercialization offices, and philanthropic partnerships are no longer nice-to-haves.

For patients: Promising treatments will die in the valley. Tempest's HCC drug is one visible casualty; the invisible ones are discoveries that never got funded to begin with.

For investors: The late-stage premium makes sense given the risk profile. But the pipeline feeding late-stage opportunities is constricting. Today's Series D abundance may become tomorrow's deal drought.

For policy: SBIR/STTR reauthorization is urgent. The House passed a 1-year extension; the Senate hasn't acted. Every month of uncertainty compounds the damage.

"Capital is a commodity. If you want to make an opportunity successful, you have to provide support." — Blackstone

The valley of death has always been biotech's defining challenge. In 2026, it's not just wide—it's becoming impassable at the bottom while the survivors at the top find abundant capital. The question isn't whether promising science will die unfunded. The question is how much.
